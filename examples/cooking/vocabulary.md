# Cooking Vocabulary

Accumulated through sessions. Newest first within each category. This is personal glossary — not a reference; it's what *you* have actually learned and tested.

---

## Technique foundations

**coconut cream separation**
When heating full-fat coconut cream in a pan, the fat eventually separates from the liquid (you'll see oil pooling at the edges, surface shimmering). This rendered fat is a frying medium. Add curry paste *after* separation — it fries rather than boils, which develops flavor completely differently. Skip this step and paste tastes raw. Logged after 2026-03-22 Thai curry breakthrough.

**blooming (spices, aromatics)**
Heating spices or aromatics in fat before adding liquid ingredients. Activates fat-soluble flavor compounds — the fragrance change is your signal that it worked. Applies to: curry paste fried in coconut cream, cumin seeds in oil, chili flakes in olive oil (like the kale pasta garlic step).

**pasta water**
Starchy, salted water left in the pot after cooking pasta. Reserve a cup before draining. It's an emulsifier — binds fat and water-based sauce components into a glossy coating, and loosens tight sauces without diluting flavor. Tap water doesn't work for this (no starch). Learned from the 2025-12-01 kale pasta failure.

**fond**
The brown (not black) residue left in a pan after searing meat. Water from the meat releases, then evaporates, leaving behind caramelized proteins and sugars. Full of flavor. Basis of pan sauces — deglaze with liquid and it lifts straight off. If it's black, you've crossed into burnt; that's bitter.

**deglazing**
Adding liquid (wine, stock, water) to a hot pan to lift and dissolve the fond. Do it immediately after removing the protein. Pour in liquid, scrape the bottom with a wooden spoon as it steams. Turns the pan fond into a sauce base.

**al dente**
Italian "to the tooth." Pasta cooked to slight firmness — still with a very thin raw core when cut. Stop 1-2 min before package time; finish in the sauce. The pasta absorbs sauce and finishes cooking in the pan. Overcooked pasta turns mushy in the sauce.

**fat calibration**
Adjusting a recipe's fat quantity to your preference and context. Restaurants use more fat than most home cooks want — it improves mouthfeel and carries flavor, but home cooking priorities differ. When testing a new recipe, read fat amounts with skepticism. Start under the recommended amount; add more if needed. Kale pasta: cut from 5 tbsp to 3 tbsp.

## Heat and doneness

**maillard reaction**
The browning of proteins and sugars at high heat (above ~150°C / 300°F). Requires dry surface — wet protein steams instead of browning. This is why you pat meat dry before searing. Adds deep savory flavor that you can't get by any other method. Not the same as caramelization (which is sugars only).

**rendering**
Cooking fat slowly out of meat (bacon, duck skin, chicken skin) by applying moderate heat. The fat melts and pools in the pan; the meat tissue crisps in its own rendered fat. Use low-to-medium heat — too high and the outside crisps before the fat can escape.

**bare simmer vs. rolling boil**
Bare simmer: occasional small bubbles breaking the surface, no vigorous movement. Right for braises, slow-reducing sauces, cream sauces. Rolling boil: vigorous, turbulent. Right for pasta water, blanching. Bare simmer is harder to maintain on gas — you may need to use two burners at different heights or a diffuser.

## Sauces

**emulsification**
Combining fat and water-based ingredients into a stable, smooth sauce. Requires either an emulsifier (egg yolk in aioli/hollandaise, mustard in vinaigrette) or vigorous mechanical mixing. Breaks when: too hot, too cold, or added too fast. Prevention: add fat slowly in a thin stream while whisking continuously.

**reducing**
Evaporating liquid from a sauce to concentrate flavor and thicken texture. "Reduce by half" = cook until volume is halved. Visual cue: thicker, slower bubbles; the sauce coats a spoon and holds a line when you run your finger through it. Don't rush with high heat — the sauce will seize or burn.

## Shopping and sourcing

**brand matters (pastes and sauces)**
For manufactured products like curry paste, the brand meaningfully changes the dish — not just heat level but flavor profile. Mae Ploy (Thai green curry paste) vs. Mae Anong: Mae Ploy has more funk, heat, and depth. Mae Anong is milder and sweeter. Switching brands mid-recipe may require other adjustments.

**Asian grocery vs. supermarket**
For authentic Thai and Southeast Asian ingredients (Thai basil, kaffir lime leaves, palm sugar, Mae Ploy paste, fish sauce), the Asian grocery is worth the extra trip. Supermarket "Thai basil" is often Italian basil; supermarket "fish sauce" brands vary widely in quality. Once you've sourced the right version of an ingredient, note the brand.
