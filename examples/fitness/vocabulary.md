# Fitness Vocabulary

Accumulated through sessions. Concepts and terms you've actually worked with — not a textbook, your glossary.

---

## Training principles

**progressive overload**
The principle of consistently increasing the demand on the body over time — adding weight, reps, sets, or reducing rest. Without progressive overload, adaptation plateaus. The challenge: figuring out the right rate of progression (too fast = breakdown; too slow = wasted capacity).

**RPE (Rate of Perceived Exertion)**
A 1-10 scale for how hard a set felt. RPE 10 = absolute max, nothing left. RPE 7 = hard but 3 reps in reserve. RPE 8 = 2 reps in reserve. RPE 9 = 1 rep in reserve. Used to regulate intensity without always testing maxes. More useful than % of 1RM for beginners because 1RM fluctuates with recovery, stress, sleep.

**e1RM (estimated one-rep max)**
Calculated from working set data rather than direct testing. Formula: `weight × (1 + reps / 30)`. Example: 75 kg × 5 → 75 × 1.167 = 87.5 kg e1RM. Use consistently for comparisons to be meaningful. Don't test actual maxes until the end of a block.

**deload**
A planned period of reduced training volume and/or intensity to allow accumulated fatigue to dissipate. Not a rest week — still training, but at ~60% of normal load/volume. The feeling of "flatness" or "restlessness" during a deload often confirms it was needed (you're not tired anymore). Schedule after every 4-8 weeks of hard training.

**deload restlessness**
The feeling of energy and mild frustration during a deload — you feel like you should be training harder. This is a sign the deload is working, not a signal to stop it. Distinct from genuine lack of motivation (which might indicate deeper fatigue or burnout). Logged after 2026-07-10 check-in.

**hypertrophy vs. strength rep ranges**
Hypertrophy (muscle size): 8-15 reps, moderate weight, higher volume. Strength (neuromuscular efficiency): 3-6 reps, heavier weight, lower volume with more recovery. Current program is strength-focused: 3-5 sets × 3-6 reps. Both produce strength gains; the distinction matters when designing accessories.

## Technique

**hip hinge**
The foundational pattern for deadlifts, RDLs, and good mornings. Hinging at the hip (not the spine) while maintaining a neutral lumbar spine. Common failure: bending at the waist instead of pushing the hips back. Cue: "push the wall behind you with your hips" or "reach for the ground with your shins, not your back."

**bracing (intra-abdominal pressure)**
Taking a deep breath into the belly (not the chest) before a heavy lift, then holding it tight. Creates a rigid cylinder around the spine. Required for heavy squat and deadlift. Without it: the spine "buckles" under load. Cue: "big breath, tight belly, then sit down."

**forward lean (squat)**
Excessive forward trunk inclination during a squat. Two root causes: (1) mobility — tight hip flexors or limited ankle dorsiflexion that forces the body forward to maintain balance; (2) technique — leaning into the bar instead of staying tall. Current root cause for this user: hip flexor tightness from extended desk time, not strength or technique failure. Treatment: couch stretch + 90/90 hip flexor work pre-session.

**Nordics (Nordic hamstring curl)**
An eccentric hamstring exercise done by anchoring the feet and slowly lowering the torso toward the floor under control. One of the best exercises for hamstring injury prevention and eccentric strength. "Eccentric only" = controlling the lowering phase, but not pulling back up (using hands on floor to reset). Start with 3-4 reps because they're brutally hard relative to what they feel like.

## Program terminology

**block**
A structured training period (typically 4-8 weeks) with a specific goal (strength, hypertrophy, etc.) and a planned progression scheme. Followed by a deload. Current: Block 1 (Strength Foundation, 6 weeks).

**linear progression**
The simplest progression model: add a fixed weight each session (e.g., +2.5 kg every session on squats). Works until it doesn't — typically for a few months for intermediate lifters. When linear progress stalls, switch to weekly progressions or RPE-based autoregulation.

**accessory work**
Exercises that support the main lifts but aren't the primary focus. Examples: RDLs supporting deadlift, face pulls supporting bench health, Nordics supporting hamstring resilience. Progressed differently from main lifts — usually on feel (RPE) rather than fixed percentage.

**posterior chain**
The muscles on the back side of the body: hamstrings, glutes, spinal erectors, calves. Often underdeveloped relative to the anterior chain (quads, chest, front delts) because sitting shortens the anterior and lengthens the posterior. RDLs and hip hinges are posterior chain work.
