# Writing Vocabulary

Accumulated through sessions. Terms for the craft of writing you've actually used — not a workshop glossary.

---

## Voice

**compression**
Writing toward clarity by removing rather than adding. A sentence is compressed when removing any word would lose meaning. The test: "what can I cut?" rather than "what can I add?" Identified as the core of this writer's voice in the 2026-03-18 session: the strongest sentences across six pieces were short, declarative, concrete. Compression is the through-line.

**emotional backup**
A section where the writer states explicitly the emotion the piece is already showing elsewhere. Usually the result of not trusting the showing — the writer hedges by saying it plainly in case the showing wasn't enough. Almost always cuttable, and cutting it usually raises the emotional register rather than lowering it. First identified in 2026-04-22 revision of "The Audit."

**voice pattern**
A recurring formal choice that, across multiple pieces, becomes identifiable as the writer's style. Not just how you wrote this piece — how you write. This writer's voice pattern from six pieces: short declarative sentences, concrete nouns, no hedging subordinate clauses. Compression is the through-line.

## Structure

**earned beginning**
An essay begins at the moment that earns the reader's interest — the most specific, alive, or strange moment that makes them want to keep reading. Everything before that moment is the writer warming up; it belongs to the drafting process, not the final piece. Cut the warm-up. Identified in the 2026-02-25 session: the actual beginning of "The Audit" was on page 2.

**context dump**
The opposite of an earned beginning. The writer lays out background and context before arriving at the live material. Natural in a first draft (you have to understand the context before writing the real thing), but rarely survives revision. Signals: the opening paragraph contains the words "I have always," "Growing up," or "For the past X years." You're warming up; the piece hasn't started yet.

**one-sentence paragraph test**
For each paragraph, write one sentence summarizing its single job. If you can't, the paragraph doesn't have a single job. If three consecutive paragraphs have the same job, merge them. Applied in the 2026-04-08 structural session: identified two jobless paragraphs and three redundant ones; essay cut by ~600 words with no loss of content.

**narrative distance**
How close or far the narrator is from the events of the piece. Close: present-tense, sensory, immediate ("The spreadsheet has 47 rows"). Far: summary, retrospective, analytical ("Over those months, I learned to track what I could not control"). Both are useful; mixing them without intention is disorienting. This writer defaults to medium-far distance and is learning to move closer in specific scenes.

## Process

**generator vs. revisor temperament**
Some writers generate easily but resist revision; others revise comfortably but struggle to generate. Neither is better, but they suggest different working strategies. For a generator: start many pieces and mine for the one with heat. Treat revision as making, not removing — you're not subtracting, you're building a tighter thing. Identified in onboarding as this writer's core tension: volume is not the problem; completion is.

**five-draft commitment**
Committing to at least five drafts before considering a piece done or abandoned. Forces passage through the resistance that arrives after draft 2-3, when the piece looks most broken. Most pieces that eventually work get worse before they get better in revision. The rule: do not evaluate on draft 2.

**finding the moment**
The process of locating, within a first draft, the specific image, scene, or sentence that is actually alive — the kernel the piece is about. Often not at the beginning. Once found, restructure the piece around it. Applied in "The Audit": the spreadsheet scene was the moment; everything else was context around it.
