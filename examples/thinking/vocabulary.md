# Thinking Vocabulary

Accumulated through sessions. Mental models and concepts you've actually used — not an aspirational reading list.

## Decision frameworks

**inversion**
Instead of asking "how do I make this succeed?" ask "what would make this fail?" Then avoid those failure modes. Useful when you're emotionally drawn to a decision and need a forcing function to surface the downsides. Applied in the 2026-04-14 conference decision: "what would have to be true for this to be the wrong call?" — changed the decision.

**pre-mortem**
Imagine the decision has been made and it went badly. Now write a plausible story of how that happened. Different from listing risks — you're already in the future, which removes the "it probably won't happen" bias. Effective for decisions with irreversible downside. Applied to the conference decision: the scenario of insufficient prep during peak-load quarter was plausible enough to change the call.

**steelmanning**
Constructing the strongest possible version of the opposing argument — not the one you could easily dismiss. The test: "would the person who holds this view recognize it as an accurate summary of their position?" Harder than it sounds. First applied in the 2026-03-15 team conflict: writing out the IC's likely perspective produced a more accurate diagnosis than direct reflection.

**second-order thinking**
Asking what happens after the first-order consequence. First order: "If I take on this project, I get visibility." Second order: "If I take it on at capacity, my other work suffers and my delivery reputation is damaged." Most decisions that look like wins have second-order costs that surface 3-6 months later.

## Forecasting and calibration

**calibration**
Whether your stated confidence levels match your actual accuracy over time. Being 80% confident and right 80% of the time = well-calibrated. Being 80% confident and right 60% of the time = overconfident. Tracked via prediction log. Current pattern: better calibrated on process/timeline predictions than interpersonal ones.

**reference class forecasting**
Before predicting an outcome for your specific situation, find comparable past cases and look at their base rates. "How long has onboarding actually taken in similar roles?" rather than reasoning from this specific person's resume. Identified as an underused model in the 2026-03-30 planning session — I kept defaulting to inside view despite committing to this.

**inside view vs. outside view**
Inside view = reasoning from the specific details of your situation ("this project has clear scope and good people"). Outside view = reasoning from historical base rates of similar situations ("comparable projects run 30% over estimate"). The outside view is almost always more accurate for timeline and project predictions. Logged after 2026-03-30 session.

**base rate**
The underlying frequency of an event across comparable cases. "What percentage of hires reach full autonomy in 6 weeks?" is a base rate question. Useful as an anchor before adjusting for inside-view details. Without the base rate, your prediction is pure inside view — you have no calibration reference.

## Biases you've caught in yourself

**confirmation bias (selective consultation)**
Seeking opinions from people who already support your preferred option. Identified as the root cause of the November 2025 job decision failure. Mitigation: before a significant decision, explicitly name "who has the strongest case against this?" and consult them.

**visibility pull**
The tendency to commit to high-visibility opportunities (conferences, cross-team projects, panels) based on the validation they offer, rather than whether you have genuine content or capacity right now. Recognized in the 2026-04-14 session. Pattern: the yes impulse comes before checking whether the substance justifies it.

**anchoring**
Letting the first number or framing you encounter disproportionately influence your final estimate. Your version: anchoring on best-case timelines for interpersonal predictions (onboarding, hiring), ignoring historical base rates that would pull the estimate longer.

**motivated reasoning**
Reasoning backward from the conclusion you want to reach, rather than following evidence forward. Distinct from confirmation bias: motivated reasoning is about how you process information you have; confirmation bias is about which information you seek. Catch it by asking: "if I wanted to reach the opposite conclusion, which of my premises would I challenge first?"
