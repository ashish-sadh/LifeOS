# Speaking Vocabulary

Accumulated through sessions. Terms for the craft of speaking you've actually used — not a presentation tip sheet.

---

## Structure

**narrative arc**
A talk with a narrative arc has a beginning that creates tension, a middle that develops it, and an end that resolves it. Distinct from a topic tour (here's what I know about X) or a findings report (here's what I found). The difference: a narrative arc makes the audience feel something is at stake. Applied in the PlatformX migration talk: "what we expected vs. what actually happened" established the arc.

**audience-facing opening**
An opening that starts with something the audience already feels, believes, or has experienced — rather than with speaker credentials, context-setting, or an agenda slide. Forces the audience to say "yes, that's true" within the first 30 seconds, which creates immediate engagement. Contrast: a speaker-facing opening starts with "Today I'm going to walk you through X" — that's for the speaker's comfort, not the audience's orientation. Registered after the 2026-04-15 prep session.

**rule of three**
Three is the minimum needed to establish a pattern and the maximum a listener can hold without a list feeling overwhelming. "Three lessons from the migration" lands as structure; "seven lessons" needs a visual aid and loses momentum. When you have more than three, compress to three themes.

**so what / now what**
A two-part test for any key message: (1) So what — why should this specific audience care? (2) Now what — what do you want them to do, think, or feel as a result? If you can't answer both in a single sentence, the message isn't clear yet. Applied to the migration talk: so what = "your estimate will be wrong, plan for that"; now what = "build a discovery sprint before committing to a timeline."

## Delivery

**filler words**
Words or sounds inserted at transitions to fill silence: "um," "uh," "so," "like," "you know." They emerge under cognitive load — when you're thinking, your mouth fills the gap. For this speaker: "so" is the dominant filler, used as a sentence-opener after pauses. Mitigation: replace with a deliberate pause. The pause is actually stronger. Tracked via weekly recording drill.

**opening-dependent anxiety**
Anxiety disproportionately concentrated in the first 90 seconds of a talk. Once past that window, delivery usually stabilizes. Implication: the opening is the highest-leverage investment per minute of rehearsal. A cold-rehearsed opening that goes smoothly can break the anxiety cycle; a stumbled opening can extend it across the whole talk.

**prosody**
The musical properties of speech: pitch variation, pace, volume, and pause. Monotone delivery — same pitch, same speed, no pauses — signals nerves or disengagement to an audience, regardless of content quality. Varying prosody signals conviction. The highest-impact single change for flat delivery.

**pacing under pressure**
The tendency to speak faster when nervous. Problems: the audience can't absorb compressed delivery, emphasis disappears, and speed signals anxiety to the room. Mitigation: build deliberate pauses into the script. Pauses force pacing even under pressure because they create physical structure the speaker has to honor.

## Audience dynamics

**Q&A tells**
Signals in Q&A that reveal how the talk actually landed. Long Q&A = engaged, good coverage. No questions = confused, cold, or topic felt closed. Questions that restate your point = message didn't land clearly. Questions that build on your point = message landed and stuck. The PlatformX Q&A produced building questions — the best indicator.

**room read**
Noticing audience behavior during a talk: phone-checking (disengaged), leaning forward (interested), writing notes (landing), cross-talk (either losing the room or excited discussion — read other signals to tell which). A skill that develops with reps. For the first dozen talks, you're too inside your own head to notice it. Getting there takes volume.
